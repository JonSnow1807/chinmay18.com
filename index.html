Distributed systems · live at mustard.watch

Mustard Watch Party

Video synchronization for watch parties, with a model-checked control protocol.

Clients connect over WebSockets, estimate a clock offset from the socket acknowledgment (NTP-style) and correct drift with predictive rate control; room state lives in Redis behind atomic Lua scripts. The 48 ms P95 is steady-state, player-reported drift across three Chrome clients at roughly 300 ms simulated RTT over a 240-second run. A separate audio-output measurement showed the player API understates true skew by about 45 ms at P95, so the number is quoted as player-reported, never as physical sync.

TLA+/TLC model checking exposed a stale-epoch bug after store resets, which led to ordered epochs; idempotency keys plus atomic updates give at-most-once application of control commands under duplication and reordering. The multi-instance path is exercised in a three-instance lab and nightly CI; production currently runs one backend instance. The Go and Rust relays are conformance studies rather than the production backend. At 10,000 local connections the Rust relay used 14.5 KB per connection against 40 KB for Go; the tail-latency comparison was inconclusive.

GPU kernels · PyTorch custom ops

Fused LayerNorm / RMSNorm CUDA operators

Residual-add fusion, FP8 outputs and a deterministic backward, with no graph breaks under torch.compile.

The eager composite runs residual-add, normalization and an FP8 cast as three kernels with HBM round-trips; the fused version does all three in one. Parameter gradients use fixed-order reductions instead of atomic accumulation, which is where the determinism comes from. The modules register through torch.library.custom_op, so compiled graphs stay whole.

For dynamic RMSNorm→FP8 in FP16 on an A100 I measured 4.9–7.2× over the eager composite and 1.04–1.76× over the compiled composite across six shapes. Those are kernel-time comparisons, not end-to-end model speedups, and the compiled-composite win does not transfer to the H100, where the repository documents 0.70–1.20× and makes no claim. The non-FP8 fused-add ops sit at parity with compiled PyTorch.

Performance tooling · on PyPI as pytorch-autotune 2.0

PyTorch AutoTune

Times candidate training configurations on the target model and keeps the fastest.

The tuner benchmarks precision, compile mode, memory format and fused-optimizer combinations on the target model and batch, under a time budget. A winner has to beat the incumbent by more than 3%, the search never returns a configuration slower than the baseline, and the report records every trial together with compilation cost and the number of training steps to break even.

Against PyTorch-default FP32 eager on an A100 the tuned stack reached 2.71× on ResNet-50, 3.84× on ResNet-18 and 6.65× on a 6-layer Transformer; against plain torch.compile it was 2.0–6.1×. With TF32 matmuls enabled in the baseline, the Transformer gain falls to 2.55×. Rebuilding the winners in a fresh training loop reproduced the committed numbers within 1.5%. Earlier versions advertised a 4.06× T4 result and an energy figure; both were retracted in the changelog because nothing committed supported them.
